The work is not about becoming less empathetic—it's about becoming more comfortable exercising appropriate authority when required.

🛠 Core Frameworks & Tools
Subject-Behaviour-Impact (SBI): Used for difficult conversations and feedback. Subject = the situation or context. Behaviour = the specific, observable actions. Impact = the consequences of those actions. Keeps conversations focused and constructive.
Behavioural Scales: Define what excellence, adequacy, and inadequacy look like for key behaviours. Provides objective language for feedback and expectation-setting. Used with Jesse on business case standards.
Professional Scepticism Questions: Has this person provided sufficient information for me to decide? What bias might they have? What is the quality of their risk analysis? Would I proceed based on this case if it were my own? What would I need to see to change my mind?
CEO Authority Principles: You get power by using it appropriately. Requiring rigorous thinking is helpful, not harsh. Professional scepticism is an organisational duty, not personal cynicism. Clarity and directness, delivered with respect, strengthen relationships.
Preparation Structure: For important conversations: write down the subject, behaviour, and impact. Anticipate emotions and defensive responses. Prepare the key question. Identify the outcome you're seeking. Allow for adaptive response in the conversation itself.

💭 Central Insight: Power That Fits Like a Glove
"You don't need to become someone you're not. But you do need to become more comfortable wielding appropriate authority as CEO. You get power by using it."
The Key Paradox: By using your authority well—being clear, direct, and appropriately demanding—you actually strengthen rather than damage relationships. People know where they stand and what's expected. Your empathy and care for people are genuine strengths. They don't need to be abandoned; they need to be supplemented with greater comfort in directness and appropriate authority.
What This Means in Practice: Setting clear expectations with Jesse about business case quality isn't harsh—it's helpful. You're teaching him how to bring proposals that will succeed. Helping Nick understand that starting with problems diminishes his influence is a gift, not a criticism. It makes him more effective. Your job as CEO isn't to master every technical domain; it's to ensure those seeking decisions provide information that enables sound judgement.
The 200-Hour Challenge: Find 200 hours per year (10% of working time) for strategic thinking, planning, and deep work. Use this time for conversations like "How do I get a step change in Jesse?" rather than firefighting and rework. Eliminate 200 hours of repetition, failed processes, and low-value activity. This isn't optional—it's essential for operating at your best.
